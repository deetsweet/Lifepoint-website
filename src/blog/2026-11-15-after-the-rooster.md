Almost everyone lives with regret. When author Daniel Pink ran a large survey on it, only one per cent of people said they never look back and wish they'd done something differently.

Failure doesn't just make us feel bad for a while. It goes after two questions we carry every day: **Am I loved?** and **Am I useful?** We start to believe we are the thing we did, and we assume that whatever God had for us, we've lost it. What we really fear isn't failure. It's being unloved and useless.

## Peter's worst night

On the night Jesus was arrested, Peter promised he would lay down his life for him. Jesus told him that before the rooster crowed he would deny him three times (John 13:36–38). In the garden Peter lashed out with a sword. Then, warming his hands at a charcoal fire in the high priest's courtyard, he was asked three times if he knew Jesus. Three times he said, "I am not" (John 18:17, 25–27). Then the rooster crowed.

A few hours earlier, Jesus had told the soldiers, "I am he." Now Peter says, "I am not."

It's worth noticing that the Bible doesn't hide this. Peter's own preaching is thought to lie behind Mark's gospel, so he told this story about himself.

## Two ways we get stuck

We usually try one of two things with failure:

- **Avoid it.** We stay busy and hope it fades. But shame grows best in secrecy and silence. King David found that keeping quiet about his sin wore him down until he confessed it (Psalm 32:3–5).
- **Replay it.** We go over it again and again, often at two in the morning. Research on rumination shows that going over it alone doesn't heal it. It deepens the groove.

## Back to the fire

Weeks after the resurrection, Peter has gone back to fishing. Jesus meets him on the shore beside a charcoal fire. The word John uses for that fire appears only twice in the New Testament: once in the courtyard, and once here (John 18:18; 21:9). Jesus chose the place.

Three times Jesus asks, "Do you love me?" Three times Peter answers, and each time Jesus gives him a task: "Feed my lambs… Take care of my sheep… Feed my sheep" (John 21:15–17). Three denials, three questions. Jesus doesn't let Peter avoid his failure, but this time Peter isn't going back over it alone. Jesus is standing with him, and he has cooked him breakfast.

## Forgiven, and sent

Between the two fires was the cross. While Peter said "I am not", Jesus told the truth and was killed for it. "It is finished" (John 19:30). Jesus is never surprised by our failure.

Forgiveness answers "Am I loved?". The calling answers "Am I useful?". Together they leave us in one of four places:

- **Ruined:** ashamed and sidelined. Peter after the rooster.
- **Repaying:** ashamed, but busy for God, trying to make up for it.
- **Relegated:** forgiven, but sure God is done with us. Peter back at his boats.
- **Restored:** forgiven and sent.

Jesus gives Peter both on the same morning. He hands his people to the man who denied him, and ends with the words that began it all: "Follow me!" (John 21:19). Weeks later Peter preached the first Christian sermon, and three thousand people came to faith. It's an old pattern. Isaiah was forgiven by a coal from the altar, then said, "Here am I. Send me!" (Isaiah 6:6–8).

## Go back to the fire

Wherever you are, Jesus wants to meet you at the place of your failure. You don't need to fix yourself first, and you won't go back alone. Grace makes a place where people with a past can have a future.

## Going deeper

Bible study questions on the key passages from this message.

### John 13:36–38 and 18:15–27 · Peter's denial

1. Compare Peter's promise in 13:37 with what he says in 18:17. What changed between the two?
2. Jesus says "I am he" (18:5) while Peter says "I am not" (18:17). What is John showing us by putting these side by side?

### John 21:1–19 · Breakfast on the beach

1. What details in verses 1 to 9 echo Peter's first call by the lake (Luke 5:1–11) and the night of his denial?
2. Why do you think Jesus asks Peter the same question three times? Why is Peter hurt (v17)?
3. Each answer is followed by a task. What does this tell us about how Jesus restores people?

### Psalm 32:1–5 · Keeping silent

1. What happened to David while he kept silent? What changed when he confessed?

### Isaiah 6:1–8 · Forgiven, then sent

1. Trace the order of events: vision, confession, cleansing, calling. Why does the order matter?
2. Where do you see the same pattern in Peter's story?
